FAQ: Choosing a Tax Advisor

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

This page answers common questions about choosing a tax advisor. The answers are general education and not tax advice. Where more detail is helpful, the answers link to longer guides on this site.

How to Use This FAQ

The questions below follow the order in which people typically think about hiring an advisor: whether to hire at all, what credentials mean, how to compare firms, what to bring to a first meeting, and what to do if something goes wrong. You can read straight through or jump to the question that matches where you are. Each answer is short on purpose, with a link to a longer guide when you want more detail.

A Few Themes That Run Through the Answers

Three themes recur. First, verify what you can. Credentials, licenses, and preparer numbers can be checked, and checking is quick. Second, prefer clarity to promises. A firm that explains its process, scope, and fees in plain language is generally a better bet than one that promises results. Third, fit matters. The best advisor for a friend may not be the best advisor for you, because your situation, your preferences, and your questions are different. Keeping these themes in mind will help you make sense of the details.

When to Ask for Help Choosing

If you are overwhelmed by choices, start small. Write down your top three needs, choose two firms to speak with, and ask each the same five questions. Then decide which conversation left you with the clearest understanding. You can always change later, and a modest first step is better than months of indecision. Remember that no single choice needs to be permanent.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I hire a tax advisor instead of using software?

Software can work well for simple returns. Consider an advisor when your situation involves a business, rental properties, multiple states, equity compensation, a sale, or a major life change, or when you want planning during the year. The more decisions you make that have tax consequences, the more an advisor may add. See Tax Preparation Firms vs Tax Planning Firms.

What is the difference between a tax preparer and a tax advisor?

A preparer compiles and files returns based on the past year. An advisor also looks ahead, helping you make decisions before they happen. Many professionals do both. Ask what a firm actually does between filing seasons.

Which credential is best: CPA, enrolled agent, or attorney?

None is best in every case. CPAs, enrolled agents, and attorneys all have unlimited representation rights before the IRS, and each has different strengths. Choose based on your needs and the professional's experience. See CPA, Enrolled Agent, or Tax Attorney: What Is the Difference?.

How can I check that someone is qualified?

Ask for their credentials and license number, then verify with the state board of accountancy, the state bar, or the IRS directory, depending on the credential. Also confirm that a paid preparer has a preparer tax identification number. See Verifying a Tax Professional's Credentials.

Should I choose an advisor who specializes in my industry?

Experience with situations like yours is helpful because the advisor will recognize typical issues. Ask about specific experience, and be careful with generic claims of expertise in everything. See Choosing an Advisor with Experience in Your Industry.

How do I compare fees?

Convert each proposal into a total for the same scope, including anything billed separately, and compare what is included. Do not compare only the base fee. See How to Compare Tax Advisory Proposals.

Are online reviews reliable?

They can offer clues, but they are hard to verify and easy to manipulate. Look for patterns, treat savings claims skeptically, and use reviews to generate questions, not verdicts. See How to Read Online Reviews of Any Tax Firm Critically.

Why does this site not publish reviews?

Reviews are difficult to verify, and presenting selected reviews on a site the firm controls would raise fair concerns. The site explains how to evaluate any firm instead. See Our Policy on Reviews and Testimonials.

What should I bring to a first meeting?

A short summary of your situation: your income sources, entities, properties, goals, and any concerns. You do not need to bring every document. The advisor can tell you what to gather. See What a Good First Meeting with a Tax Advisor Looks Like.

What should I expect the advisor to promise?

A responsible advisor promises a process, candor, and diligence, not a specific tax result. Be cautious of promises of guaranteed refunds or savings. See Why Promises of Guaranteed Refunds and Savings Are a Problem.

Do I need a local advisor?

Not necessarily. Many firms work remotely. Consider your preference for in-person meetings, the advisor's experience with your state, and how documents will be exchanged. See Remote or Local Tax Advisor: How to Decide.

How many advisors should I talk to?

Two or three is usually enough to compare approaches. More may not add much unless your situation is unusual.

What if my current advisor is not meeting my needs?

Raise your concerns first. If they persist, consider a change, and plan the transition so that records and deadlines are handled. See When to Consider Changing Tax Advisors.

Is it okay to get a second opinion?

Yes. A second opinion is reasonable for significant decisions, and a confident advisor should welcome it.

How can I protect my personal information?

Ask how the firm stores and transmits documents, use secure portals, avoid sending sensitive documents by ordinary email, and provide only what is needed. See Questions About Data Security and Privacy.

What should I do if I think an advisor acted improperly?

Gather documentation, protect yourself, and consider reporting to the relevant licensing body or tax authority. See How to Report Tax Preparer Misconduct.
