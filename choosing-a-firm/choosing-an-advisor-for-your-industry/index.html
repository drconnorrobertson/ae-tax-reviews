Choosing an Advisor with Experience in Your Industry

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Tax rules apply broadly, but the questions that matter differ by industry. A restaurant owner, a physician, a software founder, and a real estate investor face different issues. An advisor with experience in your field will usually recognize those issues quickly. This guide explains how to test for experience and how to avoid overvaluing it.

Why Industry Experience Helps

Industry experience means the advisor has seen the typical patterns: how revenue is recognized, what records are kept, which deductions and credits are common, which elections matter, and where the errors tend to occur. An experienced advisor may ask better questions and may need less explanation from you. In areas such as real estate depreciation, professional practice compensation, or multi-state operations, experience can matter a great deal.

How to Test Experience

You can learn a lot by asking targeted questions:

  • What types of clients do you work with most often?
  • What are the most common tax issues you see in businesses like mine?
  • Can you describe how you would approach a situation like mine, in general terms?
  • What information would you need from me?
  • What records do businesses like mine commonly lack?

Listen for specifics. An advisor with experience will describe concrete issues, such as documentation for participation hours or reasonable compensation analyses, and not just generalities.

Beware of Generic Claims

Statements such as we work with all industries or we specialize in everything can be honest descriptions of a general practice, but they do not tell you about experience in your field. Conversely, a firm that claims expertise in a niche but cannot describe its typical issues may be relying on marketing. Ask for concrete examples framed in general terms without client names.

Confidentiality Limits

A firm cannot share client names or details without permission, and it should not. Do not expect specific client stories. Instead, ask about typical situations and the firm's approach. A professional who is careful about confidentiality is behaving appropriately.

Where Specialization Can Be Overstated

Specialization is helpful, but it has limits. A firm may focus on one industry but lack depth in another area you need, such as state tax or estate planning. Consider whether your needs cross several areas, and ask how the firm coordinates with other professionals. Also consider that too narrow a specialization may mean the firm applies a standard playbook that does not fit your facts.

Tax Topics That Often Require Specific Experience

Some areas are technical enough that general experience is not sufficient. Examples include cost segregation and depreciation for rental real estate, the rules for real estate professionals and short-term rentals, retirement plans with employees, qualified small business stock, multi-state tax questions, and equity compensation. If your situation involves one of these, ask directly about the firm's experience.

Fit Beyond Technical Skill

Industry experience is only one part of fit. Consider communication style, availability, and whether the firm's working style matches your preferences. Some clients want a hands-on advisor who explains every step. Others want a concise summary. Ask how the firm works and decide what suits you.

A Short Checklist

  1. Write down the tax issues most likely to matter for your situation.
  2. Ask each firm how it would approach them.
  3. Compare the specificity of the answers.
  4. Ask how the firm coordinates with attorneys and other advisors.
  5. Confirm that the person who would do the work is the person you spoke with, or that they are involved.

Ask How the Firm Stays Current

Industry-specific tax issues change. Ask how the firm keeps up with changes in areas relevant to you, such as new legislation, IRS guidance, and court decisions. A firm that can describe how it tracks changes, for example through professional education, research services, or internal review, is more likely to catch updates that affect your situation. You do not need a detailed answer, but you should hear something more concrete than a general assurance.

Look for Signs of Curiosity

An advisor who asks about your business model, your customers, your property portfolio, or your goals, is showing curiosity that often leads to better advice. An advisor who moves quickly to a standard solution without asking questions may be applying a template. Curiosity about your facts is a good sign in any industry.

A Short Case for Asking About Boundaries

Ask each firm what kinds of matters it would refer to someone else. A firm that knows its boundaries and has referral relationships with attorneys, actuaries, or other specialists is usually more reliable than a firm that claims to handle everything. Referral relationships are a sign that the firm recognizes where its expertise ends, which is exactly the quality you want when your situation involves several specialties.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I only work with a firm that specializes in my industry?

Specialization helps, but it is not the only factor. Consider experience with your specific issues and the quality of communication.

Can a firm share client examples?

Not identifying ones without permission. Ask about typical situations instead.
