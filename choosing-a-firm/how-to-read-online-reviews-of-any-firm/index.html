How to Read Online Reviews of Any Tax Firm Critically

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Evaluation Guides / Choosing a Tax Advisory Firm / How to Read Online Reviews of Any Tax Firm Critically
No reviews on this site. This site does not publish client reviews, ratings, star counts, or testimonials. It explains how to evaluate a tax advisory firm, what to ask, and how AE Tax Advisors approaches engagements.

Online reviews are a common starting point when people look for a tax professional. They can be useful, but they also have serious limitations. This guide explains how to read reviews of any tax firm critically, and why this site does not publish reviews of AE Tax Advisors or anyone else.

Why Reviews Are Hard to Interpret

Reviews reflect an individual's experience, and that experience depends on many things: the person's expectations, their situation, the timing, and their communication with the firm. A tax outcome that seems poor to a client may result from the law, not from the advisor's work. A very positive review may come from someone who was pleased by an early refund that later turns out to be problematic. Reviews rarely contain the details needed to judge the substance of the work.

Verification Is Limited

Most review platforms cannot fully verify that a reviewer was actually a client. Some platforms use verification steps, but they are imperfect. Reviews can be written by competitors, by friends, or by people who were never clients. Some firms solicit reviews selectively, and some offer incentives, which platform rules and consumer protection rules may restrict. Some reviews are removed and others are not.

Look for Patterns, Not Stories

A single dramatic review says little. A pattern of similar comments across many reviews says more. Notice whether several reviewers mention the same strengths or problems, such as responsiveness, clarity of communication, unexpected fees, or missed deadlines. Give more weight to comments that describe specific, checkable facts than to general praise or anger.

Read the Middle

Extremely positive and extremely negative reviews are the least informative, since they are often driven by emotion. Moderate reviews that describe both strengths and weaknesses tend to contain more useful detail.

Check How the Firm Responds

How a firm responds to criticism can be revealing. A calm, factual response that offers to resolve the matter is a good sign. A defensive or dismissive response is a reason for caution. Also note that firms are limited in what they can say publicly about a client's situation because of confidentiality obligations, so a short response may reflect that restriction rather than indifference.

Be Wary of Quantities and Claims

Be cautious of profiles that show large numbers of perfect ratings with little detail, or that display dollar figures of savings. Savings claims in reviews are especially unreliable, since they cannot be verified, and the circumstances of one client rarely apply to another. This site does not publish savings figures or ratings for that reason.

Separate Service from Outcome

Consider whether a review is about the service, such as communication and organization, or about a tax outcome. Service comments are more transferable to your situation than outcome comments. A comment that the firm explained things clearly and met deadlines tells you something about the process. A comment about the size of someone's refund tells you very little.

Cross-Check with Other Sources

Use reviews alongside verifiable evidence: credentials, professional discipline records, the clarity of the engagement letter, and your own experience in a first conversation. Consumer protection agencies and licensing boards can also provide information about complaints, though the availability of complaint data varies.

A Simple Approach

Treat reviews as a prompt for questions, not as a verdict. If a review raises a concern, ask the firm about it. If a review praises something, ask how the firm delivers it. Then decide based on what you learn.

Consider the Reviewer's Situation

When a review does contain detail, ask whether the reviewer's situation resembles yours. A review from a person with a simple return says little about how a firm handles a business sale or a portfolio of rentals. A review from someone with a complex situation may be more relevant, but only if it discusses the actual service. Also consider timing: a review written years ago may not reflect the firm's current team or process.

Do Not Rely on Reviews for Technical Quality

Clients are usually not in a position to judge the technical quality of tax work. They may not know whether a deduction was missed or whether a position is aggressive. That is why credentials, process, and documentation are better evidence of technical quality than satisfaction ratings, which mostly measure service experience.

A Practical Way to Use Reviews in Your Process

If you decide to read reviews, set a limit. Look at reviews on a couple of platforms for a firm, note recurring themes in a short list, and turn each theme into a question to ask the firm. For instance, if several reviewers mention slow responses, ask about the firm's response times and how it manages busy periods. If several mention clear explanations, ask how the firm presents recommendations. That approach turns reviews from a verdict into a source of useful questions, and it reduces the influence of any single dramatic story.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I ignore online reviews entirely?

Not necessarily, but treat them as one weak input among several and verify the important things yourself.
