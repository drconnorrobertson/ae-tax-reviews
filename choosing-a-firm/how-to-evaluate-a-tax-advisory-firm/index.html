How to Evaluate a Tax Advisory Firm

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Evaluating a tax advisory firm is easier when you break the decision into parts. Instead of asking whether a firm is good in general, ask whether it is a good fit for your needs, whether it is qualified to do the work, whether its process is clear, and whether its claims can be checked. This guide provides a framework you can use with any firm, including AE Tax Advisors.

Start with What You Need

Before comparing firms, write down what you need. Are you looking for return preparation, proactive planning, help with an IRS notice, entity setup, retirement plan design, or specialized work such as real estate depreciation? Firms differ in what they emphasize, and a firm that is excellent at one kind of work may not be the best choice for another. A clear statement of your needs makes every later step easier.

Check Qualifications

Qualifications are the easiest thing to verify and the most commonly skipped. Ask who will work on your matter, what their credentials are, and how you can confirm them. Certified public accountants are licensed by state boards of accountancy. Enrolled agents are licensed by the IRS. Attorneys are licensed by state bars. Anyone who prepares returns for pay must have a preparer tax identification number. The guide on Verifying a Tax Professional's Credentials explains how to check each one.

Understand the Process

A good firm can describe its process in plain language. What happens after the first conversation? What information does it need? How are recommendations presented? Who implements them? How often will you hear from the firm? If the answers are vague, ask for more detail. A clear process is a good sign, since it suggests the firm has thought about how to deliver its work.

Look for Specialization That Matches Your Situation

Tax law is broad. A firm that regularly works with owner-operators or real estate investors will usually understand the common issues in those areas. Ask how much of the firm's work involves situations like yours, and ask for examples of the kinds of questions it handles. Be careful with claims of expertise in everything. See Choosing an Advisor with Experience in Your Industry.

Evaluate Communication

Communication is a large part of the experience. Ask how quickly the firm responds, who your main contact will be, and whether you will have access to the person doing the analysis. Notice how the firm communicates during the sales process. Prompt, clear, and honest communication before you hire is a reasonable predictor of communication afterward, though not a guarantee.

Read Proposals and Engagement Letters Carefully

A written proposal or engagement letter should describe the scope of work, the fees, the timeline, and what is not included. If it does not, ask for one that does. Compare it with other proposals on the same points. See What an Engagement Letter Should Cover.

Be Careful with Promises

A firm that promises a specific tax result before it knows your facts is making a claim it cannot support. Tax outcomes depend on the law, on your circumstances, and on documentation. A trustworthy firm talks about possibilities, risks, and conditions, not guarantees. See Why Promises of Guaranteed Refunds and Savings Are a Problem.

Use Reviews Wisely

Online reviews can offer clues, but they are difficult to verify and easy to manipulate. Treat them as one data point and look for patterns, not individual stories. This site does not publish reviews. Instead, see How to Read Online Reviews of Any Tax Firm Critically for how to read them critically anywhere else.

Ask for References Carefully

Some firms offer references from clients who agreed to speak. That can be useful, but remember that references are chosen by the firm. Ask what the reference used the firm for, and whether their situation resembles yours. Also respect confidentiality: a firm should not share information about clients without permission.

Trust Your Own Assessment

After a few conversations, ask yourself whether you understood what was said, whether your questions were answered directly, whether the firm seemed interested in your situation, and whether you would be comfortable calling the firm with a problem. Your judgment about fit is an important input.

A Simple Scorecard

Consider scoring each firm from one to five on these dimensions: qualifications you verified, clarity of process, relevant experience, communication, clarity of the written proposal, and your comfort level. The scores are not scientific, but the exercise forces you to compare firms on the same criteria instead of on impressions.

Keep Notes and Compare Later

After each conversation, write a short summary while it is fresh: who you spoke with, what they said about scope, process, and fees, and any concerns. Note anything you would need to verify. When you compare firms a week later, your notes will be more reliable than your memory, and they will help you avoid being swayed by whichever conversation happened most recently. If a firm sends a proposal, file it with your notes so that all the information about each option sits together.

Know Your Own Constraints

Finally, be realistic about budget, timeline, and how involved you want to be. A firm that fits your budget and timeline, and that matches how much you want to participate, is often a better choice than one that looks impressive but does not fit those constraints.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many firms should I talk to?

Two or three is usually enough to compare approaches, though it depends on how complex your situation is.

Is the lowest price the best choice?

Not necessarily. Compare scope, qualifications, and process along with price.
