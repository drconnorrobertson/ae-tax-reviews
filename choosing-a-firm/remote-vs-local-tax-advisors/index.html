Remote or Local Tax Advisor: How to Decide

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Many taxpayers now work with advisors they have never met in person. Others prefer someone nearby. Neither approach is inherently better. The right choice depends on your needs, your comfort with technology, and the nature of your tax situation. This guide describes the main trade-offs.

Advantages of Remote Advisors

Working remotely widens your choices. You can find an advisor with specific expertise regardless of location, and you can schedule meetings without travel. Documents can be shared securely online, and many advisors provide portals for uploading documents and signing forms. For clients with busy schedules or with operations in several places, remote work can be more convenient.

Advantages of Local Advisors

A local advisor may know the local market, local government rules, and regional business practices. For issues such as property taxes, local licensing, or local real estate markets, that knowledge can help. Some clients also prefer face-to-face meetings, which can build trust and allow easier discussion of sensitive matters.

State Tax Expertise

Federal tax rules are the same everywhere, but state rules differ. A remote advisor may be less familiar with your state's rules unless the firm has experience there. Ask whether the firm regularly works with clients in your state and how it handles state filings. Also ask whether the firm relies on local professionals for state-specific matters. This is especially important if you operate in more than one state.

Licensing and Authority

Professionals must be authorized to practice in the relevant jurisdictions. Federal tax return preparation and representation before the IRS follow federal rules. Some state licensing rules apply to the practice of accountancy or law, and they may affect what an out-of-state professional can do. Ask how the firm handles licensing for your state, and verify credentials. See Verifying a Tax Professional's Credentials.

Communication

Regardless of location, communication is central. Ask how you will communicate: video calls, phone, email, messaging, or a portal. Ask about response times and about who your contact is. Remote relationships work well when expectations are clear. Local relationships work well when the advisor is accessible. See Questions About Communication and Service.

Security and Privacy

Remote work involves sharing sensitive information electronically. Ask how the firm protects your data: encryption, secure portals, access controls, and policies for staff. Avoid sending sensitive documents by unsecured email. Ask what happens to your documents when the engagement ends. See Questions About Data Security and Privacy.

Document Handling

Remote firms typically rely on scanned or uploaded documents. Ask how originals are handled, how long records are kept, and how you can retrieve them. If you have a large number of physical records, a local advisor may be more convenient, or you can scan them.

Representation and Meetings

If an examination or meeting with a tax authority becomes necessary, a professional can generally represent you remotely, using a power of attorney, though some agencies request in-person meetings. Ask how the firm handles this, and whether additional fees apply.

Culture and Fit

Some people find remote relationships impersonal, while others find them efficient. Consider your own preferences. Try a first conversation by video and see how it feels. Fit matters more than location.

Where AE Tax Advisors Fits

You can start with a discovery call and see whether the format works for you. Ask about how the firm works with clients, how documents are exchanged, and how state-specific issues are handled.

A Short Checklist

  1. Decide whether in-person meetings matter to you.
  2. Ask about experience with your state.
  3. Confirm licensing and credentials.
  4. Ask about security practices.
  5. Confirm how documents and meetings are handled.

A Practical Test

If you are unsure, try a low-stakes trial. Schedule an introductory video call and see how the conversation goes. Ask a question you already know the answer to and see how it is handled. Ask about the process for exchanging documents and for signing. If the experience is smooth and clear, remote work may suit you. If you find yourself wishing you could sit across a table, a local advisor may be the better choice.

Time Zones and Availability

If the firm is in a different time zone, ask about working hours and response times. A difference of a few hours is rarely a problem, but it can affect scheduling around deadlines, so confirm that expectations match.

When a Hybrid Approach Makes Sense

Some clients use a local professional for local matters, such as property taxes, licensing, and bookkeeping, and a remote firm for specialized planning. That approach can combine local knowledge with specialized expertise, provided the two communicate. If you use more than one advisor, designate one as the coordinator and share contact details so that decisions and documents move smoothly. Clear roles prevent duplicate work and conflicting advice, and they make it easier to hold each professional accountable for their part.

Ask About Backup Coverage

Ask what happens if your main contact is out for an extended period. Remote and local firms alike should have a plan for continuity, so that deadlines are not missed when someone is unavailable.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a remote advisor less reliable than a local one?

Not inherently. Reliability depends on the professional and the firm, not the location.

Can a remote advisor handle my state return?

Often yes, but ask about their experience with your state.
