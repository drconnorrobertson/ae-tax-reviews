Tax Advisory Pricing Models Explained

By AE Tax Advisors | Educational guide | Updated September 2026

Tax advisors charge in several ways, and the model affects incentives, predictability, and how you should compare proposals. Understanding the common models helps you ask better questions and avoid surprises. This guide describes them in general terms. It does not state what any firm charges, and it does not provide price ranges, since fees vary widely by firm, location, and scope.

Per-Return Pricing

Preparation is often priced per return or per form. A basic individual return may be priced differently from a return with business schedules, rental properties, or multiple states. The price generally reflects the number and complexity of forms. Per-return pricing is predictable for a defined task, but it may not include planning, and additional forms or complications can increase the price.

Hourly Billing

Some advisors bill by the hour. You pay for the time spent, at a rate that may vary by the seniority of the professional. Hourly billing can be fair for uncertain or open-ended work, but it can be hard to predict. Ask for an estimate and for updates as time accumulates. Ask whether junior staff and senior staff have different rates, and how time is recorded.

Flat Project Fees

A flat fee is a fixed price for a defined project, such as an entity analysis, a cost segregation coordination, an amended return, or a retirement plan design. The advantage is predictability: you know the price in advance. The disadvantage is that the scope must be clear, since work outside the scope may be billed separately. Ask what is included and what triggers additional fees.

Retainers and Subscriptions

A retainer is a recurring fee, monthly or quarterly, in exchange for a defined package of services, which might include planning meetings, estimated payment reviews, and access to the advisor. This model supports year-round planning. The disadvantage is that you pay whether or not you use the services, so you should understand what is included and how often you will actually use it.

Hybrid Models

Many firms combine models. For example, a firm might charge a flat fee for preparation, a retainer for ongoing planning, and hourly rates for special projects such as responding to notices. Ask how each piece is priced and how they fit together.

Fees Tied to Results

Some advisors propose fees tied to a percentage of tax savings or refunds. These arrangements deserve extra caution. Rules governing practice before the IRS generally restrict contingent fees for preparing original returns, with limited exceptions. Even when permitted, fees tied to savings create an incentive to overstate benefits or to take aggressive positions. Ask how savings are measured and who decides. See Why Promises of Guaranteed Refunds and Savings Are a Problem.

What Affects the Price

Whatever the model, several factors influence price:

  • The number of entities, properties, and states.
  • The complexity of transactions and elections.
  • The quality of your records.
  • The level of professional involved.
  • The frequency of meetings and communication.
  • Deadlines and rush requests.
  • The extent of implementation help.

If a proposal does not explain these drivers, ask.

Comparing Models

To compare proposals with different models, convert them into a comparable annual cost for the scope you need. For example, convert an hourly estimate into a total for the expected hours, and add any separate preparation fees. Then compare what is included. A lower price with a smaller scope is not necessarily better than a higher price with a broader scope. See Flat Fee, Hourly, or Retainer: Which Suits You?.

Watch for What Is Missing

Look for items that may be excluded, such as state returns, extensions, responding to notices, amended returns, and meetings beyond a certain number. Missing items may be billed separately. Ask for a list of exclusions.

Questions to Ask

  1. Which pricing model are you proposing, and why?
  2. What is included and what is excluded?
  3. How will changes in scope be handled?
  4. When and how will I be billed?
  5. What happens if I end the engagement early?

A Note on Predictability

Predictability matters to many clients. If you dislike surprises, prefer models that give you a fixed number for a defined scope and ask for notice before any change. If you are comfortable with variability and want the flexibility to ask questions as they arise, hourly or hybrid models may suit you. Being honest about your own preference helps you select a proposal that fits.

How Pricing Reflects Value Delivery

Pricing models can also reveal how a firm thinks about its work. A firm that prices planning as a defined process with deliverables is signaling that it has a structure. A firm that quotes only an hourly rate may be signaling flexibility. Neither is wrong, but the difference is worth noticing when you compare firms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is one pricing model better than the others?

Each has advantages. The best fit depends on your needs, the predictability of the work, and how much support you want.

Should I be wary of very low fees?

Compare scope and experience. A low fee may reflect limited scope, and you should confirm what is included.
